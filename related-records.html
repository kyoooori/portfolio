Related Records

Teaching permits, licenses and complaints to talk to each other. A year-long, greenfield parent-child feature for OpenGov’s permitting software.

Overview

In OpenGov’s permitting software, everything is a record: a permit, a license, a complaint. But at a high level, those records did not talk to each other. A building permit could not know that its electrical, plumbing and mechanical permits were still waiting, and staff had to keep track of it themselves.

Customers had been asking for this through every channel we had, from our ideas portal to sales and professional services calls. It had sat on the roadmap for a while but kept falling off. As OpenGov moved up-market, having records share information became table stakes. I designed Related Records, a way to link records as parents and children, automate those links, share information between them and sequence their workflows.

Problem

We failed with larger communities that use PLC across departments. A construction project needs a primary permit and several child permits. A music festival can need a dozen. People had built clever workarounds because the product gave them no way to relate records:

  • Projects did not work. “A project to me is nothing more than a parent.” It was a tag at the top of a record that you still had to apply by hand to everything.
  • Workflows were siloed. Parent records have to wait for child permits: “We only issue the C of O (Certificate of Occupancy) after other permits on the property have been issued.” Staff could not see that from the record itself.
  • Dependent work was hard to find. The Location tab only listed records at the exact same address.
  • Dependencies were tracked by hand, with sticky notes, phone calls and emails about which permits a project required.

Research

I started with what the company already knew: interviews with customer success, professional services, implementation and sales engineering, comments on our ideas portal, and recordings of past sales calls. Then I went to users, with 24 interviews across three rounds, averaging 58 minutes each and covering ten communities. Participants came from recommendations by professional services, customer success and project managers, and from volunteers who opted in at our quarterly webinar.

My interview script started from the gaps in what we knew. I wrote down everything I wanted to learn in discovery, synthesized in Miro with a lot of tagged data, and wrote up the results.

Key insight: a family of needs, not one feature

Five use cases covered nearly everything we heard: special event permits that need many linked permits, construction projects with a primary permit and child permits, retail and business permits at mall, building and unit level, integrations that turn permits and inspections into tasks in an asset management system (snow removal was one example), and combined fees so an applicant pays once.

Behind them sat five goals: required records, permit hierarchy, bulk permit and license tracking, task dependencies, and payment aggregation. The insight was that this was not one feature. Trying to solve everything at once led to some thought paralysis on my side, so I broke the problem down before designing anything.

I split users into four groups, the quick-linkers, the organizers, the builders and the designers, each with its own goal and example scenario. Then I listed tangible tasks, started with user journeys, and mapped how records would talk to each other before opening a screen.

Planning: pause, then buckets

After the research I paused to plan. I grouped the work into four buckets, a word I used a lot: ad-hoc linked records, automated linked records, form mapping, and enhanced projects. Each milestone tracked research status, design status, engineering status and a design confidence score, so we could see where the risk was.

Design

I broke the design into micro-journeys: displaying related records, creating a relationship, relationship templates and projects.

Displaying related records

I compared a graph, a tree and a list, and a tree-and-list hybrid. The tree was overwhelming at scale, and the list was easier to understand. For where related records should live, I tested a separate tab against showing them inside the workflow, in the interviews themselves.

Where I landed: a separate tab with a count, quick filters with a legend (information shared, blocks this record, waiting for this record), the upstream record called out, and icons that show each relationship at a glance. The row metadata is reused from Global Search, so a record looks the same wherever you meet it. I also designed for records with more than one parent, for opening a record’s details and its relationship from the list, and for mobile.

Creating a relationship

To create a relationship, we needed the user to tell us five things: which record type, under what conditions (only for automated relationships), who the users are, whether to copy information, and whether the workflows depend on each other. I explored a page against a modal and finalized a guided modal flow. The empty state suggests records at the same location to quick-add. Then four steps: when the relationship should be created, which users to add (users from the parent are copied by default, to give people a head start), what information to copy, and whether there are dependencies between the records, using the language staff already use.

Automation and templates

An automation can be set up in settings, with conditions for when a child record should be created, and forms can be mapped so that field entries in a parent are shared with its children.

Projects, later

Our users are creative, so I held projects back on purpose. I wanted to see how people used Related Records before we put a bigger concept on top of it. In the meantime I prototyped a project dashboard to keep the conversation going.

Results

This project is greenfield, and many of the improvements are qualitative, so I won’t dress it up. The measures below are where we are, and I’ve noted what is not measured yet.

It worked in users’ hands. I ran a usability test with five participants on five tasks, covering adding a new related record, quick-adding, adding an existing record as related, navigating the tab and editing a relationship, and editing a workflow blocker. Then a developer and I went to a customer user group, where a customer volunteered to set up a related record live. She did it smoothly, talked through the experience, and understood the interface.

A measurement plan. We expect more ad-hoc related records, more relationship templates in larger communities, fewer combo-permits, fewer phone and email questions about requirements, and faster processing of permits and licenses. What we can measure today is the number of workflow steps per record and the number of change requests, both ideally falling. We do not yet measure time to complete a workflow step. Usage tracking runs through Fullstory.

What I’d do differently

Ask the UX team for feedback earlier. I could have proactively sought more design feedback, which would have helped me refine faster.

Respect the scale. With four Figma files and hundreds of screens, this was a very large undertaking. I’d break it down more aggressively from the start.

Document edge cases as I go. I’d keep an edge-case log alongside the designs, not after.

What I’m glad about: when the PM changed mid-project, the engineering lead and I kept the team stable and the work moving. And watching a customer move through a flow we had been nervous about, without trouble.
