Designing A Connection Between Government Records To Reflect Real-World Relationships

A net-new way for permitting staff to connect records that used to live in silos, so a building permit knows about the electrical, plumbing and inspection work that depends on it.

Overview

In OpenGov’s Permitting & Licensing product, everything is a record: a permit, a license, a code complaint. But the records did not talk to each other. A building permit had no idea that an electrical permit was waiting on it, or that a Certificate of Occupancy could not be issued until both were done.

Customers had asked for this through every channel we had: Productboard, our ideas portal, sales calls and professional services meetings. Competitors already offered it. It had sat on the roadmap for a while and kept slipping, but as OpenGov moved up-market, connected records became table stakes. I was the designer on it from discovery through launch.

What we already knew

Before talking to users, I started with what the company knew. I interviewed customer-facing staff across customer success, professional services, implementation and solutions engineering, read through ideas portal comments, and listened to past sales calls. Four themes came up:

  • We struggled with larger communities that use PLC across several departments.
  • Customers wanted trees of records, with hierarchy and sequencing.
  • Users were inventing clever workarounds to connect records.
  • Required records were being sorted out over phone calls, which took time.

Research

I ran 24 interviews over three rounds with staff from 10 communities. Participants came from two places: recommendations from professional services, customer success and project managers, and volunteers from PLC’s quarterly customer webinar. I wrote the interview script by listing the gaps in what we knew, and I asked people to share their screens and show me how they handled connected work today.

I synthesized everything in Miro: tagged notes, a summary for every interview, and maps of how each community’s records related to one another.

Use cases

  • Special events: a music festival needs several permits and licenses handled together.
  • Construction projects: a primary building permit is the parent, and electrical, plumbing and mechanical permits are its children.
  • Retail and business: permits at the mall, building and unit level need to be tracked and related.
  • Existing systems: inspections and permits become tasks in an asset management system, to trigger work like snow removal.
  • Fees and payment: fees across several permits combine into one payment.

Problem

Interviews showed exactly where the current system broke down.

Projects did not work

PLC already had a Projects feature, but users saw it as little more than a label.

“Projects is just a tag at the top. It’s not worth the fact that you still need to go in and tag everything.”Customer interview

Workflows were siloed

A parent record often has to wait on its children, but nothing in the product connected their workflows. Checking a related record’s status took six clicks.

“We only issue the C of O (Certificate of Occupancy) after other permits on the property have been issued.”Customer interview

Dependencies lived on sticky notes

The only way to find related records was the Location tab, which only showed records at the same exact address. Everything else was tracked by hand, sometimes on sticky notes on a monitor.

“We need more conditions beyond yes/no to determine what permits will be required.”Customer interview

Framing the problem

The problem was big enough to cause some thought paralysis. To break it down, I split users into four groups by what they were trying to do: the Quick-Linkers, the Organizers, the Builders and the Designers. Then I listed concrete tasks for each group and mapped them as user journeys, which led to the core question: how should records talk to each other?

Planning in buckets

After quick, rough explorations, I paused to plan scope with the team. We split the work into four buckets and started with the first:

  • Ad-hoc linked records
  • Automated linked records
  • Form mapping
  • Enhanced projects

Within that, I broke the design into micro-journeys: displaying related records, creating a relationship, relationship templates and projects.

Design: displaying related records

I compared a graph, a tree and a list. A full tree was overwhelming. A tree combined with a list was much easier to understand. I also had to decide where related records would live: in their own tab, or inside the workflow. I tested both in interviews.

Where I landed: a separate tab with a count; quick filters that double as a legend; the upstream record pinned at the top; metadata reused from Global Search; and icons that show each relationship at a glance. I also designed for mobile and for records with more than one parent.

Design: creating a relationship

To create a relationship, the user needed to tell us five things: which record type, the conditions (for automated relationships only), who the users are, what information to copy, and which workflows depend on each other. After weighing a page against a modal, I landed on a guided setup:

  • The empty state suggests records the user might want to quick-add.
  • Picking a record type happens before the setup, not inside it.
  • Step 1 sets when the relationship is created, right on the parent’s workflow.
  • Step 2 copies users from the parent record by default, to give a head start.
  • Step 3 picks which information to copy.
  • Step 4 adds dependencies between workflows, with quick actions in the language users already use.

Validation

I ran five usability tests with five tasks each, using remote control so participants worked in the prototype themselves. Then the lead developer and I went to a customer user group in Worcester, MA, where a customer volunteered to set up a related record live. We were nervous. She set it up smoothly, talked through it as she went, and understood the UI.

Measuring success

This was a greenfield project, and many of the improvements are qualitative, so the metrics are still a work in progress. What we expect to see:

  • Ad-hoc related records being created
  • More relationship templates in larger communities
  • Less use of combination permits
  • Fewer calls and emails about requirements
  • Faster processing of permits and licenses

What we can measure: fewer workflow steps per record, fewer change requests, and time to complete a workflow step, tracked in Fullstory.

What’s next

Relationship templates and projects come next. Our users are creative, so we want to see how they use Related Records before we introduce projects. I’ve built an initial prototype and run a DIY project dashboard exercise with users.

Reflection

What went well

  • Collaboration: our PM changed mid-project, and the engineering lead and I kept the team stable and the work moving.
  • Iteration: four Figma files with hundreds of screens. Seeing where we started and where we ended up makes me happy.
  • Users understood it: in live testing, the customer flew through setting up a relationship.

What I’d do differently

  • Ask the UX team for feedback earlier and more often. It would have helped me refine designs faster.
  • Break a very large project into smaller releases sooner.
  • Document edge cases as I go.
